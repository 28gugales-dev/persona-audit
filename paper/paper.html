Silent misses: auditing a belief-mapping tool on philosophers' documented changes of mind

Soham Gugale (Amphi)
With Apart Research
Research conducted at the AI x Epistemics Research Sprint (Apart Research), November 2026

Abstract

A tool that shows people where their views have changed can look safe while missing what it exists to find. We audited Persona, a belief-mapping tool we had shipped, against 30 changes of mind that authors documented in their own published writing. Given each whole history, the shipped prompt raised no false alarm and found 3 of the 30. We isolated four causes: grouping posts by shared words, a definition that counts only views held at the same time, a 400-character excerpt cut, and retractions that never restate the old view. A rewritten prompt, frozen before two later test sets were sourced, found 10 of their 12 changes where the shipped prompt found 2 (p = 0.008). The deployed rewrite held up with author names and dates masked, on changes the model cannot state from memory, and on a second model (11 against 1 of 12). On a fourth set, sourced after Persona's current prompt shipped and run once, it found 6 of 7 changes and the shipped prompt 1. Its edge over a one-line naive prompt, which found 20 of 30, is precision: 88% of its cards were judged genuine, against 50%. Persona now runs it. The general lesson is that a tool validated only on false alarms hides its misses; our own first scorer hid most of the repair's effect until we checked it. We report every prediction, including the 35 of 75 that failed. Whether surfacing these changes helps people decide is unmeasured.

1. Introduction

Track 3 asks whether AI tools lead people to decisions they later endorse. One candidate reads what a person has written over the years, finds where their views conflict or have changed, and asks where they stand now: the "reflection scaffolding" of Forethought's angels-on-the-shoulder sketches [1]. Done well, it surfaces drift people miss, including views picked up from an assistant that agreed with them [2, 3, 4]. Done badly, it nags about refinements, or silently drops the changes people would most want on record. The second failure is the dangerous one, because nothing shows it: a tool that flags nothing raises no complaints.

We audited one such tool that was already shipped. Persona, part of Amphi (amphi-forum.org), imports a person's posts, notes and AI-chat exports and sends excerpts to Claude Haiku 5.5, which returns pairs that become cards with one neutral question. Before this sprint it had never raised a false alarm in our checks. The product predates the sprint (Table 1); the sprint's work is the audit and the repairs. Contributions:

  1. A silent failure, measured on real records. On 30 documented changes of mind in four sets, the shipped prompt found 3 even when shown each whole history, and flagged no control; the shipped grouping never placed the two ends of a change together. The same harness finds a planted reversal in 29 of 30 of these histories, so the misses are the prompt's.
  2. Its four causes, each isolated (Table 3): word grouping, the prompt's definition, a 400-character excerpt cut, and retractions that never restate the old view. Three strict prompts written independently by GPT for few false alarms found 1-6 of 30, so the failure is not peculiar to our wording.
  3. A repair tested on records it was not tuned on. A rewritten prompt, frozen before two later sets were sourced, found 10 of their 12 changes where the shipped prompt found 2, with no control flagged; on Sonnet, 11 against 1; on a fourth set sourced after Persona's current prompt shipped, 6 of 7 against 1. Its edge over a one-line naive prompt is precision more than recall. Persona now runs it (Table 1).
  4. A general lesson and an audit method. A zero false-alarm rate was our evidence that the tool was safe; it was the symptom. The method: answer keys from documented changes, blind labellers calibrated on decoys, predictions committed before each run (35 of 75 failed), test sets sourced after deployment, and a check of the scorer itself: our first one, crediting only keyed passages, reported 8 of 19 pairs where the tool finds 11 of 12 cases. Section 5 turns this into a five-step checklist.

2. Related work

Sycophancy and belief drift. Assistants agree with users' stated views [2], affirm both sides of a moral conflict about 48% of the time [3], and leave people surer they are right while being preferred [4].

Contradiction and belief change. ContraDoc studies self-contradiction inside long documents [5]; BeliefShift benchmarks how assistants track a user's changing beliefs [6]; FactTrack gives facts validity intervals so change over time is not mistaken for error [7]; BeliefBank keeps a model's own beliefs consistent [8]; people prefer explanation-guided revision [9]; stance datasets follow opinion shifts on social media [15]. These model revision. We test a shipped tool end to end on real authors' documented reversals, and show that a deployed prompt's wording, its grouping step and its excerpt length can each put revision out of reach.

Prompt and cue sensitivity. Outputs shift with meaning-preserving formatting [12]; models often fall back on internal knowledge rather than supplied label definitions [13]; they mishandle negation [14]. Here the model followed its definition; the definition was the problem.

Epistemic products. Track 3's examples include a Crux Mapper for inconsistencies in group beliefs; Persona is the individual, longitudinal analogue. Reviewers of a winning Apart entry (LidaSim) asked for validation against real outcomes [11]. Our nearest substitute is writing by real people whose changes of mind are documented in their own words.

3. Methods

3.1 System under test

  1. Theme sort (on device). TF-IDF terms per entry, grouped into themes by shared words.
  2. Model step (opt-in). Per theme, up to 20 excerpts spread over time, each cut to 400 characters, go to Haiku with the production prompt (Appendix B). It has a definition ("Count a pair only when the person asserts both as their own view, on the same point, and they clash"), an exclusion list ("Do not count: ... questions, doubt ...; describing, quoting or answering someone else's view; ..."), an empty-list default ("Most themes have none, and an empty list is the right answer then") and output rules.
  3. Card, labelled "change of mind" or "live" (label added Nov 13, during the sprint).

Since Nov 14 a history short enough goes to the model whole in one call, every post cut to an equal share of 120,000 characters (at most 1,000); longer records still take the theme sort. The gate was 120 posts, which 29 of our 37 test histories (123-124 posts) exceeded; after this audit it is 124, the largest history we ran whole (main cea9afd9).

3.2 Answer keys

Real writing (main). Documented changes of mind in public-domain English texts (Gutenberg, Wikisource, archive.org), each with a secondary source, every passage checked by script to appear verbatim. Each case has an early passage stating the earlier view, a passage in a later, separate work where the author announces the change, a later unannounced statement of the new view where one exists, and an early-work control. Each case becomes a history: up to 60 paragraphs sampled from the early work and 60 from the later one, dated, plus the passages (72-124 posts). Four sets:

  • Development (12 histories, 10 authors): Hume, Peirce, Nietzsche x3, Kant, Russell, Darwin, Newman, Mill, Spencer, Huxley. Six were the held-out set of earlier rounds.
  • Fresh set 1 (6), sourced by a separate agent with no access to prompts or results: Franklin, Romanes, Godwin, W. James, Freud, Gladstone. Used to diagnose the repairs, so not untouched.
  • Fresh set 2 (6), sourced by another agent after the configuration was frozen and deployed (cases written 14 minutes after deployment), run once: Lyell x2, Besant, Darwin (Glen Roy), Buckland, Sedgwick. Its brief asked for passages near the start of their paragraph, which favoured the shipped 400-character cut (cause 3).
  • Fresh set 3 (6), sourced the next day by a third agent blind to how the tool reads text, asked for announcements that retract without restating the new view (5 of 6 do); brief and predictions committed before the cases existed; run once: Newton (slave trade), Coleridge (Unitarianism), Miller (the days of Genesis), Ruskin (Rubens), Frazer (totems), Wordsworth (representation).
  • Fresh set 4 (7), sourced by a fourth agent after Persona's current prompt shipped, with no hint about how the tool reads and no constraint on retraction type; brief, predictions and cases committed before any run; run once: Johnson (fixing the language), Weismann (variability of unicellular organisms; two volumes of one title, essays six years apart), Wilde (pain and pleasure), Jane Harrison (primitive religion), Schliemann (the burnt city at Troy), Martineau (a future life), Murchison (the Tilestones). Four are OCR scans whose errors are kept verbatim. It is reported apart from the 30 used everywhere else.

Whether each announced passage states the new view or only retracts ("I neither know how to correct my former opinions") was coded before any run (Appendix E).

Planted items (mechanism only). 24 GPT-written histories on five contested PhilPapers questions [10], 50 hand-written items, 10 philosophers' reversals paraphrased, and 40 items written after the prompt repairs were chosen; blind Sonnet labellers agreed with the intended labels on 149/150 and 40/40 (Table 2).

3.3 Scoring

Strict pairs (earlier rounds): a run counts only if it returns the keyed early passage with the keyed later passage. By change of mind (case level): a run finds a case if it returns a keyed pair, or any pair with one side from the early work and one from the later work that two labellers, Haiku and Sonnet, each shown the case's question and the two passages and blind to which prompt produced them, both judge to reject or abandon the earlier view on that question. Over fourteen label rounds the labellers called 362 of 397 judgments of the 51 keyed pairs and 10 of 240 random early-by-later decoy pairs a match (pooled with round 31's looser batch, 15 of 270), and 3 of 51 hard negatives (each case's control passage against its later passages; two were announcements that disown the whole earlier book). A third judge, Opus 5.5, required to quote the rejecting words verbatim, confirmed 105 of the 116 off-key pairs that carry credit but accepted only 42 of the 51 keyed pairs (round 26). A case is found when a majority of runs (or, for two-call configurations, of looks) find it. Controls flagged count against a configuration. The contrast does not depend on the labellers: crediting only the exact key pair, with no labeller, the shipped prompt finds 2 of 30, the naive prompt 11 and Persona's prompt 15; crediting any early-by-later pair that includes a keyed passage, 4, 22 and 25 (an unpredicted check).

3.4 Runs and process

Model and harness. Haiku 5.5 through Claude Code subagents, one fresh agent per request; prompts passed verbatim, replies parsed by the production parser. Temperature cannot be set, so real-text arms ran 3-6 times. Each look of a two-call configuration pairs one run of each prompt. Development and fresh set 1 used the rewrite's tested text; on fresh sets 2 and 3 the two-call configuration used the deployed text, which adds the shipped output rules (a neutral question, at most 8 pairs); round 19's single-prompt arms used the tested texts.

Prompts. Production: the shipped prompt. Rewrite (realX in code and appendices): a generic "find contradictions" prompt with production's exclusion list, plus one paragraph saying a view the writer formerly held counts. Changes: asks first for each later excerpt where the writer turns against an earlier view of their own, even vaguely ("my former opinions") and without stating the new one, paired with the excerpt that states it, then for opposite sides a year or more apart. Paragraph: the rewrite's retraction paragraph and clause. Naive: "Where does this person contradict themselves?" Full texts in Appendix B.

Process. Each design and its numeric predictions were written in PLAN.md before its outputs existed and, through round 17, committed before them (Appendix C); rounds 18-20's plan entries were committed together after their runs, so for those rounds the record is the deployed commit that froze the configuration before fresh set 2 existed. Choices between configurations followed a rule written before the candidates' runs: most cases on development plus fresh set 1, then fewer control flags, then lower cost. Statistics: Wilson intervals, exact McNemar tests, bootstraps over histories (Appendix D).

4. Results

4.1 The shipped tool finds almost none of the documented changes, silently

Grouping. The word-overlap theme sort put the early and later passage in one theme for 0 of 19 keyed pairs in the development histories. Authors do not reuse their words across decades ("acquainted" in Russell's 1912 view, "widely held" in his 1921 retraction): ranked by TF-IDF similarity to the early passage, the true later partner came a median 59% of the way down its history, and shared no content word with it for 8 of 19.

The prompt, even with both ends present (Figure 1). Given each whole history in one call, the production prompt found 1 of 12 development cases (majority of three runs), 0, 2 and 0 of 6 in the three fresh sets, returning 0.0-0.3 pairs per history on average. It flagged no control. Under the strict score it surfaced 0 of the 19 development pairs in each of three runs. It is not the harness: with one planted reversal added to each history, it returned the planted pair in 29 of 30 and still found 1 documented change.

2. The definition (planted items, oracle setting). With the production prompt Haiku found every unsignalled planted reversal (36/36). Crossed ablations (three runs per cell) trace the loss to the definition "asserts both as their own view" and the empty-list default: a time-anchored definition adds 16.7 [11.6, 21.9] of the 36 marked items, dropping the default 14.0 [10.5, 17.7], a placebo rewording of the same length −2.3 [−5.8, 1.3]. The real-text failure is not: the same three found 1, 6 and 1 of 30. On real text (400 characters) the definition matters less than the rewrite's paragraph counting retractions and references back, with its clause that a view the writer once held is their own: with them the shipped prompt found 19 and the edited one 21; the rewrite without them 13, with them 25. (We had predicted ≤ 12.)

3. The 400-character cut. In fresh set 1 the decisive words sat past character 400: Godwin's announcement is cut before what he would modify (it stops at "Not that I see cause to make any change respecting the principle of justice"), and James's early passage at "The private minds do not agglomerat". Excerpts of min(1000, 120,000 / posts) characters recover Godwin for both prompts and James for the changes prompt (Table 3).

4. Retraction without restatement. Hume's announcement ("I neither know how to correct my former opinions, nor how to render them consistent") names no view. The rewrite never paired it with the bundle-theory passage it retracts (0 of 9 runs: 3 at 400 characters, 6 longer); the changes prompt did in 2 of 3 runs at 400 characters but 0 of 4 longer. Every configuration Persona has run since misses it. Told which passage is the retraction and asked what earlier view it disowns (14 retraction-only cases, longer excerpts, three runs), Haiku named a matching early passage in its top three for 11: so for most retractions, Wordsworth's included, the failure is noticing the retraction, not linking it. Hume (1 of 3 runs), Kant (0 of 3) and Coleridge (1 of 3) fail even then.

On the unseen sets. The rewrite was frozen before fresh sets 2 and 3 existed. On them it found 10 of 12 changes where the shipped prompt found 2 (8-0, exact McNemar p = 0.008; set 3 alone, 4 against 0, p = 0.125). A two-prompt configuration deployed with it tied it (10 of 12), so we removed the second prompt (Table 1). Every prompt missed Coleridge, whose early view sits inside a political address, and Wordsworth, whose 1821 retraction names nothing. The deployed prompt held up under three further checks, each with predictions committed first: with author names and own-work titles replaced and dates shifted to 2001, it found the same 10 and the shipped prompt none (p = 0.002); counted by author, it won for 7 and lost for none (sign test p = 0.016); and on Sonnet 5.5 instead of Haiku, Persona's current prompt found 11 of 12 and the shipped prompt 1 (p = 0.002; a safety classifier blocked 4 of 72 runs, changing no case). On fresh set 4, the one set sourced after Persona's current prompt shipped, it found 6 of 7 and the shipped prompt 1 (5-0, p = 0.06; missed Murchison, whose retraction is in a noisy two-column scan); across all 19 cases sourced after deployment, 16 against 3 (13-0, p = 0.0002), though sets 2 and 3 were in view when the current prompt was chosen, so only set 4 is a clean test of it.

Reading, not remembering. The authors are famous. From author, years and question alone Haiku stated 14 of the 30 changes; Persona's prompt found 14 of the 16 it could not state and 12 of the 14 it could, while the naive prompt fell from 11 of 14 to 9 of 16. With the post announcing each change deleted from the history, Persona's prompt still found 16 of 30 (5 of 12 unseen). Its recall edge over the naive prompt is small: at 400 characters it found all of the naive prompt's 20 and 5 more (exact McNemar p = 0.06).

What it costs. The shipped prompt is not a precision trade-off but dominated: with the retraction paragraph it keeps its 0.07 non-genuine cards per history and finds 19 cases, not 3. The rewrite's edge over the naive prompt is precision: 88% of the cards Persona now shows were judged genuine against 50% (38 points higher [25, 51]; 0.90 fewer non-genuine cards per history [0.57, 1.23]; bootstrap over histories), and the "changed their mind?" prompt matched its recall at three times its non-genuine cards. On writing with no documented change (each early work alone, redated over six years), the deployed text raised the most false cards of any prompt, 1.10 per history. One line caused it, "Return at most 8 pairs, the sharpest first": the model filled the quota. Without the line it found 26 cases (losing Russell, gaining James) with 0.27 non-genuine cards per history on real histories and 0.40 on no-change writing, and Persona dropped the line under a rule written before the test (Table 1; its 0.67 control flags are Spencer in 2 of 3 runs). The cost does not vanish: on real two-work histories with the documented change removed, it raised 0.60 non-genuine cards per history (naive 1.17) beside 0.97 genuine ones; with no clear change to report, it reaches for weaker pairs. Requiring a verbatim quote for every card, tested as a product change under a rule written first, cut these to 0.47 but lost two cases (24 of 30, 9 of 12 unseen) and raised false cards on real histories (0.30 against 0.17 for the current prompt in the same batch), so Persona kept its prompt.

4.4 The audit's own failure: what the scorer hid

Our first scorer, used through round 17, credited a run only for the keyed later passage. Under it, the best single call (the rewrite) found 8 of 19 development pairs (majority of three runs; 5-10 per run), and we wrote that the tool remained weak. Reading its outputs showed it often paired the early view with another later paragraph stating the same change (Newman's "the Church of Rome was the only true Church"), or the announcement with other early paragraphs (Franklin's pamphlet). Scored by change of mind with blind labels, the same replies find 11 of 12 cases. Within the same round, the strict scorer also led us to conclude, and then withdraw, that the changes prompt added nothing. Separately, 12 of 36 harness subagents in one round declined the task and replied in prose containing "ok", which our runner accepted until a missing-file check caught it. An answer key built from passages is necessary but not sufficient: a change of mind is expressed in many paragraphs, and the scorer must be checked against the outputs it scores.

5. Discussion and limitations

What this means for epistemic products. A tool meant to help people see their own changes of mind missed the clearest ones, changes the authors put in print, and the failure was silent: its false-flag rate was zero throughout, which we had read as evidence of safety. Three of its four causes were reasonable-looking design choices (word grouping, a strict definition, short excerpts for cost). Only an end-to-end test on records with known changes revealed them. Any reflection tool tuned to avoid nagging will drift this way unless its misses are measured as carefully as its false alarms.

What generalises: an audit checklist for builders. The specific clauses do not generalise; these tests do, and each caught something here.

  1. Check that both ends of a known change reach the model together: word themes joined 0 of 19 keyed pairs.
  2. Plant a reversal with and without a sentence announcing it: our prompt went from 36 of 36 to 11 of 36.
  3. Run a one-line naive prompt on real records beside yours: 20 of 30 against the shipped 3.
  4. Read past the excerpt cut on every miss: Godwin's and James's decisive words sat after character 400.
  5. Score by the change, not the passage you keyed, calibrate labellers on decoys, and keep a set the configuration has never seen: our first scorer reported 8 of 19 pairs where blind labels show 11 of 12 cases.

Limitations (full list in Appendix A). No users: not shown that people want these cards or that they improve decisions. 30 real cases by famous authors (Haiku states 14 from memory; Persona's recall is no lower on the other 16), English, 18th-20th-century prose; fresh set 2 proved easy. Case-level credit rests on Claude labellers (calibrated, but not people). One model family (Haiku; Sonnet on the unseen sets only), run by the product's author. Histories of 72-124 posts; records longer than Persona's 124-post gate still take the word-theme path, which never joined a change's two ends, so for long records the failure in Section 4.1 stands until grouping is fixed.

Future work. A consented user study (protocol in Appendix A), long real records, a cross-family replication, human labels.

6. Conclusion

On documented changes of mind in real writing, Persona's shipped prompt found 3 of 30 even given each whole history, raised no false alarms, and its word grouping never put the two views together. The causes were a word-based split, a strict definition, a 400-character cut and retractions that never restate the view. A rewritten prompt, frozen before two later test sets existed, found 10 of their 12 changes with far fewer false cards than a naive prompt, and is what Persona now runs. Blind spots like these hide behind zero false alarms; real records with known changes of mind find them, provided the scorer is itself checked.

Appendix A. Limitations and Dual-Use / Ethical Considerations

Limitations, and how each would change the reading.

  1. No user study. We measure whether the tool surfaces changes of mind, not whether surfacing them helps people reach decisions they later endorse. The real-text sets stand in for a person's record and for endorsement: the authors endorsed their change in print, not our flag. If people do not want narrated changes shown, the fix is a regression for them.
    • Protocol for next time: consenting users list their known changes of mind before running, rate each card ("would I want this shown?", "is it fair?"), and are re-asked a month later.
  2. Synthetic and historical text. GPT wrote the planted posts: short, English, about philosophy. The real-text sets are published 18th-20th-century prose by famous authors, sampled from books, not posts or chats; 30 histories, 12 of them (fresh sets 2 and 3) never used to choose anything. A modern person's history is shorter per entry, noisier and mixed. The planted decoys are cleaner than real look-alikes, so the 0/96 false-flag rate is optimistic; real-text cards from the grouping-v2 pipeline were judged non-genuine in 7-9 of 25.
  3. One model family, in an agent harness.
    • The production call uses the API with effort "medium" and a real system prompt. Here the prompt reached Haiku inside a subagent, as text.
    • A harness check with a different wrapper and production's effort setting reproduced the contrast (36/36 vs 15/36), but individual borderline items flip between runs (20/36 marked items agree), so the size of the effect in production is uncertain. The direction was consistent across all phrasings, sets and wrappers.
    • The harness agents had file access in a repository that holds the answer keys. None of 1,582 real-text replies mentions a key, case file or repository path, and no agent that wrote a scored reply opened a case file, answer key or label map; two dev-set replies of the changes prompt came from agents that first read the opening of PLAN.md (round 25).
    • Sonnet 5.5 reproduced the drop and the repair, but it is the same family. A cross-family check was planned and cut when the free and subscription quotas for GLM and DeepSeek ran out. We did not use paid capacity.
  4. Contamination and small n. Haiku named the author of 9 of the 10 paraphrased cases, and of 26 of the 27 passages from the first 13 real cases shown without names (it took Huxley's 1862 passage for Darwin). Yet from author, years and question alone it stated the documented change for only 7 of those 13 cases, and four of the eight pairs realX found on the first 13 cases under the strict score are in cases it could not recall, so memory does not explain what is found; with author names, own-work titles and dates masked, the rewrite found the same 10 of 12 unseen cases (round 25), though the prose itself stays recognisable. This should make the real reversals easier to find, so the production prompt's misses are not explained by it; the repairs' recall may be inflated by it. The unseen-case comparisons rest on 12 cases (fresh sets 2 and 3), one of them easy; grouping v2 was tuned on the 6 development histories, and both of its held-out predictions narrowly failed (grouping v1's real-text prediction also failed; Appendix F). The whole-history comparison was added after our mock review asked for it, with three runs per history; its realX arm was predicted before each run (the first threshold equalled the naive baseline's already-seen score), and realX itself was tuned on development pairs shown alone, not on whole histories. Model grouping with the shipped prompt was not run on real text, so "neither fix alone" rests on the two cells we did run (word themes with any prompt; whole histories with the shipped prompt). Our real histories are 72-124 excerpts; a person's years of posts and chats are longer, which is where grouping, not the single call, has to work.
  5. The fix.
    • The fix line was written by the author after seeing failures and frozen before held-out runs (commit times, Appendix C). Because it names the cue, its recovery on marked text is partly instruction-following.
    • The cue-free rewrite avoids this, but was chosen after the ablations, then tested on fresh items, the hand-written sets and end to end (Tables 5, 7).
    • The two-call configuration (realX + changes, longer excerpts; deployed Nov 14-15) was chosen on development and fresh set 1 and tested once, on fresh set 2. Its effect on real users is unmeasured. On development and fresh set 1 it ran with the tested realX text; on fresh set 2 with the deployed text, which adds the shipped output rules (a neutral question, at most 8 pairs). The changes prompt was written by the author from failure analysis on development data. Round 19 found that realX alone, changes alone and a naive prompt also find 5-6 of 6 on fresh set 2, so that test shows the shipped prompt's failure, not that each part of the repair is needed. After round 21 we removed the second prompt from the product (Nov 15, main c9a32b4c); Persona now runs realX with longer excerpts alone (26 of 30 cases here). On fresh set 3, sourced without that bias, the two-call configuration tied realX alone (4 of 6 each), as did changes alone and the stance prompt.
  6. Scorer and labellers. Case-level credit rests on two Claude labellers judging pairs against each case's question (keyed pairs 362/397, random decoys 10/240, hard negatives 3/51 called a match; these are repeated judgments of 51 distinct keyed pairs, which drew 48, 44, 47 and 46 both-yes in four rounds). They are calibrated, not human, and a decoy rate near 5% means a configuration returning many cross-era pairs could earn some credit by chance; ours returned 2-6 pairs per history, and crediting only each run's first pair (round 32) leaves Persona now at 24 of 30, the naive prompt at 18, the shipped prompt at 3. The strict score is reported beside it. Our controls were not clean: two flagged "controls" were real changes on other questions.
  7. Round 18's record. Its plan entries were written before each run but committed together afterwards (commit c8fc24d9); the timestamped evidence for the final test is that the configuration was committed and pushed to main (72823441 / 34bfd61e, 17:24 ET) before fresh set 2 was written (17:38) or run (17:40). Fresh set 2 mostly announces the new view outright (5 of 6), its sourcing brief favoured passages within the first 400 characters of their paragraph, and three of its texts are OCR. Fresh set 3's predictions (e083b001, 02:18 ET Nov 15) and cases (cc168613, 02:51) were committed before its first run; its Ruskin case pairs two volumes of one title, and its Miller and Wordsworth secondary sources are weak.
  8. Judge. We corrected four of our own planted labels after the judge disagreed. We report the uncorrected κ (0.69), and the main neutrality claim rests on lean-vs-neutral agreement, which the correction does not affect.

Dual-use and ethics.

  • Profiling. Mapping a person's inconsistencies is a profiling capability. Pointed at someone else, the same pipeline is a tool for opposition research, harassment ("you said the opposite in 2019"), or for targeting persuasion at beliefs that are already wavering. Persona's design limits this:
    • it imports only accounts the person connects;
    • the model step is opt-in, under a separate consent;
    • the default detector runs on device and sends nothing;
    • excerpts are aliased, and nothing is stored server-side;
    • a weekly allowance caps use. These controls reduce misuse but do not prevent someone from uploading another person's public posts.
  • Steering. A reflection tool can steer while appearing to inform. We measured question neutrality (97%) for that reason. The kind label is a framing risk: labelling any pair a year apart a "change of mind" frames the newer view as growth before the person has said which they hold. This happened for 17 of the 36 unsignalled test positives, purely by date (a count that follows our chosen gaps).
  • Data used in this paper. No personal data was used. Test text is synthetic, paraphrased from published philosophy, or quoted from public-domain texts (pre-1930 publications and translations, from Project Gutenberg, Wikisource and archive.org); PhilPapers figures are used as aggregates under CC BY-NC. We did not use classroom data or anyone's Reddit history. A planned consented study of friends' histories was not run.
  • Effect of the fix. It surfaces more cards. More cards could mean more pressure on people to "resolve" views they are happy to hold loosely. Persona lets people mark a pair "not a conflict" or "both", and those choices should stay one tap away.

Appendix B. Prompts and the one-line fix

Production system prompt (apps/api/src/features/persona/conflicts.ts, CONFLICTS_SYSTEM), as shipped; the fix line, shown in bold, was not in it and was tested as an added line:

You read excerpts one person wrote over time on one theme. Find where two of their own claims cannot both stand. Count a pair only when the person asserts both as their own view, on the same point, and they clash. A change of mind still counts: if a later excerpt asserts, as their current view, something that cannot stand with what an earlier excerpt asserted, include the pair even when they say they have changed their mind. Do not count: different points or a change of subject; questions, doubt, or being less sure than before; one excerpt simply being more careful than the other; describing, quoting or answering someone else's view; jokes. Most themes have none, and an empty list is the right answer then. Return at most 3 pairs, the sharpest first. [...format and injection-defence instructions omitted; full text in the repository...]

The rewrite (Section 4.2) makes two edits. It replaces the sentence "Count a pair only when the person asserts both as their own view, on the same point, and they clash." with:

Count a pair only when each excerpt, at the time it was written, stated the person's own view, on the same point, and the two clash.

and deletes "Most themes have none, and an empty list is the right answer then." The ablation "definition only" makes the first edit alone.

Placebo definition (Section 4.2), reworded to the rewrite's length but keeping the production meaning:

Count a pair only when the person asserts both excerpts as their own view, about the very same point, and the two of them clash.

Changes prompt (deployed beside realX; CHANGES_SYSTEM in the same file):

Below is everything one person has written, oldest first. Find where they changed their mind. 1. Example cards from the rewrite (Haiku, first run; production missed all three):

  • 2019 "I would take both boxes..."; later "I have come round to taking only the sealed box when the predictor has an excellent record. I used to insist on taking both..." → "Do you hold that you should take both boxes, or only the sealed box, when the predictor is excellent?"
  • "I think numbers exist independently of us"; later "I no longer think numbers are things existing somewhere independently of us" → "Do you hold that numbers exist independently of us, or that they do not?"
  • "...a belief is justified when it comes from a way of forming beliefs that usually gets things right"; later "I changed my mind about what ultimately supports a belief..." → "Do you think a belief is justified by a reliable method, or only by basic beliefs that do not borrow support from others?"

Each question is neutral, but it asks which view the person holds when they have already said. For narrated changes, a card that confirms ("You moved from X to Y; is that still where you stand?") may fit better than one that asks. That is a design question for the user study.

Neutrality.

  • A GPT judge labels each tool-written question as neutral, leaning earlier, leaning later, or off target.
  • On 30 hand-labelled real questions plus 12 planted leaning ones, lean-vs-neutral agreement was 41/42 (κ = 0.94).
  • Four-label κ was 0.69, our primary figure. Four of our planted labels had the direction wrong; correcting them gives 0.90.
